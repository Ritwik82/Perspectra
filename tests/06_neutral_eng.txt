Trying to understand the relationship between remote work and productivity

Over the past couple of years, I have heard very different accounts of how working from home affects people's working lives. Some people I know say they can concentrate better at home because they avoid commuting, have more control over their schedules, and can organise their day around tasks that require sustained attention. Others say that working remotely makes it harder to separate work from personal life, ask quick questions, or maintain a sense of connection with colleagues.

What I find interesting is that these experiences do not seem to divide neatly according to job title or seniority. Two people doing similar work can describe completely different experiences. One may find a quiet home environment ideal for completing difficult tasks, while another may struggle with distractions, limited space, or the absence of direct interaction with teammates.

There are also different ways of defining productivity. An employee might complete more individual tasks in a day but contribute less to informal collaboration. A team might produce the same amount of work while employees report greater satisfaction. Conversely, people might feel more productive because they have fewer interruptions, even when the actual quality or quantity of their work has not changed.

I have also wondered how much of this depends on the organisation rather than the location itself. A team with clear goals, good documentation, and thoughtful communication practices might function well remotely, while another team might struggle because important decisions depend on conversations that happen spontaneously in an office. But I do not know whether this explains most of the differences people report.

Another question is whether the experiences of people who voluntarily choose remote work tell us much about what would happen if the same arrangement were imposed on everyone. People who prefer office environments may be less likely to select remote jobs in the first place, so the experiences of existing remote workers might not represent the experiences of the entire workforce.

I have not collected systematic data, and the examples I have heard may not reflect broader patterns. I am interested in what researchers actually measure when they study remote work, how they distinguish employee satisfaction from performance, and what evidence would help explain why the same working arrangement can produce such different outcomes for different people.
